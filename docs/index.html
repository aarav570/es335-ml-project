ES 335: Machine Learning (2026) / Dhvanil Shah (25110105) & Aarav Malde (25110003) / GitHub repository

Condition-based maintenance on simulated turbofan engines

On 20 held-out simulated turbofan engines, replacing each unit when a 30-cycle trailing-sensor model triggers an alarm cuts mean maintenance cost by 68.8% (calibrated logistic regression, 0 failures) to 76.2% (gradient-boosted trees, 1 failure) compared with the best fixed replacement schedule (179 cycles) at a 100:1 failure-to-waste cost ratio.

Scope note: All findings come from simulated NASA C-MAPSS FD001 runs (single sea-level regime, single compressor fault mode), a 20-engine held-out test set, and assumed failure-to-waste cost ratios — see Limitations.

How to read this page: skim the sentence below each section heading for a 60-second overview, open the Details blocks for formulas and full tables, and use the interactive explorer in Results to test cost ratios and alarm thresholds.

Question

We test whether strictly causal trailing sensor telemetry up to operating cycle t warns of turbofan degradation early enough to beat a fixed-interval replacement schedule on unseen engines.

Table 1. Problem framing and prediction-time information boundaries
Research question Can sensor history up to cycle t predict remaining useful life and raise a binary alarm early enough to reduce total maintenance cost below a fixed replacement cycle K?
What one row represents One engine (unit_id) at the end of one operating cycle t, paired with features computed strictly from cycles 1..t of that same engine.
Targets (a) Continuous Remaining Useful Life (RUL), piecewise-capped at 125 cycles; (b) Binary alarm A_t = 1 if true uncapped RUL ≤ w cycles (primary w = 30; sensitivity tested at 15 and 50).
Prediction time Immediately after cycle t completes, before deciding whether to keep the engine flying for cycle t + 1 or pull it for shop maintenance.
Available information Cycle number t (age), 3 operating settings, and 21 sensor channels recorded up to and including cycle t; zero future cycles and zero post-cutoff maintenance logs.
Not claimed Real airline fleet accuracy, physical root-cause fault causation, or operational deployment readiness.

Unplanned turbine failure in service is disproportionately expensive and hazardous compared with retiring a part slightly early. Yet engine lifetimes in NASA C-MAPSS FD001 span more than a twofold range (128 to 362 cycles), meaning any single fixed replacement interval either fails on short-lived engines or discards dozens of healthy cycles on long-lived engines.

Headline regression RMSE or classification accuracy alone cannot tell us whether sensor monitoring pays off operationally. A useful model must trigger an alarm within a practical planning window (w = 30 cycles) without crying wolf mid-life, and it must beat both an age-only timer and the best development-tuned fixed schedule across plausible failure-to-waste cost ratios.

Remaining useful life (RUL)

The number of operating cycles remaining until simulated failure: T_e - t, where T_e is engine e's final recorded cycle. Capped at 125 cycles during training because early-life wear is indistinguishable from normal manufacturing scatter.

Lead-time horizon (w)

The operational warning window before failure. Setting w = 30 cycles reflects scheduling maintenance within 30 flights (~15.2% positive rows); we also verify w = 15 (7.8% positive) and w = 50 (25.0% positive).

Details — Target formulation, cost rule, and configuration

At each cycle t of engine e with failure cycle T_e, the continuous target is clipped piecewise linearly and the binary alarm indicator marks the final w cycles:

RUL_uncapped(e, t) = T_e - t
RUL_capped(e, t) = min(RUL_uncapped(e, t), 125)
Alarm(e, t) = 1 if RUL_uncapped(e, t) <= w else 0

Under a maintenance policy that pulls engine e at its first alarm cycle t_alarm, per-engine cost equals c_fail if no alarm fires before failure (t_alarm ≥ T_e) and c_waste * (T_e - t_alarm) otherwise. Configuration parameters live in config.yaml and target attachment in src/data.py.

Data-quality findings on the 80 development engines

  • Ten uninformative raw channels: All 3 operating settings reflect fixed sea-level conditions; 6 sensors (sensor_1, 5, 10, 16, 18, 19) have zero variance and sensor_6 toggles only between 21.60 and 21.61 psia (std = 0.0014). We drop all 10 using development-fold variance checks only, retaining 14 informative sensors.
  • Heterogeneous physical scales: Core speed (sensor_9) averages 9,066 rpm while bypass ratio (sensor_15) averages 8.44, requiring fold-isolated standardization for linear models.
  • Cycle-to-cycle Gaussian noise: Single-cycle readings fluctuate noticeably relative to early-wear drift, motivating trailing-window smoothing.
  • Clean benchmark idealization: Zero missing observations, dropouts, or right-censoring appear in the run-to-failure trajectories—making FD001 cleaner than any airline sensor log.

Preprocessing

Every learned filter and scaler runs inside a scikit-learn Pipeline fit on training folds only, converting 14 noisy sensors into 43 strictly causal trailing-window trend features.

  1. Screen constant channels per training fold (SensorVarianceFilter): Drops the 3 sea-level operating settings and any sensor with training-fold std < 0.01, removing the 7 constant/near-constant channels without peeking at validation or test engines.
  2. Group by unit_id and enforce trailing windows (TrailingWindowFeatureTransformer): Computes features for row (e, t) exclusively over that engine's cycles [max(1, t - k + 1), t] with primary window k = 30 cycles, preventing future look-ahead or cross-engine bleed.
  3. Extract smoothed level, drift slope, and instability: For each of the 14 retained sensors, computes rolling mean (noise suppression), rolling standard deviation (volatility), and OLS linear drift slope (wear rate), then appends current cycle age t to form 43 features.
  4. Stabilize early cycles (t < 30): Uses expanding causal windows over cycles 1..t at the start of an engine's life while dividing slope numerators by the full 30-cycle denominator so early noise does not explode slope estimates.
  5. Standardize inside linear folds (DataFrameStandardScaler) & cap RUL at 125: Applies zero-mean unit-variance scaling strictly inside training folds for regularized linear models, and caps training RUL targets at 125 cycles because early operation shows no measurable degradation trend.
Trailing vs. centered windows

A centered rolling window uses cycles t + 1 .. t + 15 that have not occurred yet at prediction time. Strictly trailing windows ensure zero temporal leakage.

How mild is scaler leakage?

In a controlled check (sensitivity_summary.csv), fitting StandardScaler on all 80 dev engines vs. fold-isolated folds changed Ridge CV RMSE from 15.298 to 15.301—negligible here, unlike row-splitting leakage, yet still prevented by Pipeline discipline.

Details — OLS slope formula, boundary rule, and causality unit tests

For a trailing slice of length m = min(t, k) with index x = [0, 1, ..., m − 1], weights are variance-stabilized by the full-window denominator:

denom_full = k * (k^2 - 1) / 12 (for k = 30, denom_full = 2247.5)
slope(e, t) = sum((x_i - mean(x)) * s_i) / denom_full (for t = 1: mean = s_1, std = 0, slope = 0)

Automated checks in scripts/run_features.py (saved in feature_metadata.json) pass three assertions: perturbing future cycles > t leaves features at ≤ t unchanged, concatenating engines produces identical per-engine matrices, and permuting row order leaves outputs invariant.

Baseline and methods

We climb a six-rung complexity ladder from a non-sensor fixed timer and age-only models up to regularized linear trends and nonlinear tree ensembles, testing whether added complexity earns its keep.

Table 3. Six-rung model comparison ordered from simplest baseline to nonlinear tree ensembles.
Rung Model Features Core assumption Why included in ladder
Rung 0 Mean RUL / Never-Alarm Floor 0 All units share the training-set mean RUL or zero-alarm base rate. Establishes the zero-learning floor against which every model must improve.
Rung 1 Fixed Replacement Schedule (K*) 1 (timer) Engine failure risk depends only on a single fleet-wide cycle age cutoff. Operational maintenance baseline representing practice without onboard telemetry.
Rung 2 Age-Only Ridge & Logistic Regression 1 (cycle) Remaining life decreases linearly (or alarm log-odds grow monotonically) with cycle age. Isolates how much predictive power comes from calendar age alone before adding sensors.
Rung 3 Instantaneous Sensors + Ridge / Lasso / Logistic 15 Current-cycle sensor snapshots plus age linearly explain degradation state. Tests whether raw instantaneous readings beat age without temporal smoothing.
Rung 4 Trailing Trends (k=30) + Ridge / Lasso / L2 Logistic 43 Linear combinations of smoothed levels, drift slopes, and volatility track wear and well-calibrated log-odds. L2 handles collinear sensors; L1 Lasso enforces sparsity; Logistic yields calibrated alarm probabilities.
Rung 5 Trailing Trends (k=30) + Random Forest & HistGradientBoosting 43 Piecewise axis-aligned splits capture nonlinear thresholds and sensor interactions inside the training range. Tests whether nonlinear interactions outperform linear trend combinations for RUL and alarm policy.

Moving up the ladder tests a specific hypothesis at each step rather than jumping straight to complex models. Comparing Rung 3 to Rung 4 quantifies the value of 30-cycle trailing trends over raw snapshots, while comparing Rung 4 to Rung 5 measures whether nonlinear tree interactions justify losing linear interpretability.

Feature inspection on development folds shows strong physical agreement across model families: Lasso keeps 23 of the 43 features, while Logistic Regression and HistGradientBoosting both rely heavily on cycle age (cycle), trailing HPC outlet temperature level (sensor_3_mean_k30), trailing HPC static pressure drift (sensor_11_slope_k30), and trailing fuel-flow ratio drift (sensor_12_slope_k30).

Collinearity: L2 vs. L1

Because turbofan pressures, temperatures, and rotor speeds move together, unregularized OLS weights oscillate wildly. Ridge (L2) shrinks correlated groups stably; Lasso (L1) zeroes redundant channels.

Tree extrapolation limits

Decision trees predict bounded averages of training leaves and cannot extrapolate past observed feature ranges, whereas linear models extrapolate smoothly when sensors drift beyond prior extremes.

Evaluation

We partition engines—not rows—into 80 development engines (5-fold GroupKFold) and 20 sealed test engines, locking every threshold on development data before running one final evaluation with engine-level bootstrap intervals.

Table 4. Evaluation metrics and why each matches the predictive maintenance problem.
Task Metric One-line justification
RUL regression RMSE & MAE (cycles) Measures average cycle prediction error across the full trajectory and within the critical RUL ≤ 50 zone.
RUL regression NASA Asymmetric Score Penalizes dangerous late over-predictions (exp(+d/10) − 1) much more steeply than early conservative warnings (exp(−d/13) − 1).
Binary alarm (w=30) PR-AUC & Brier Score Evaluates rare-event ranking under 15.2% class imbalance without relying on misleading raw accuracy, plus probability calibration.
Maintenance policy Mean Cost per Engine Translates first-alarm triggers into operational cost: c_fail if failure occurs before alarm, else c_waste × remaining_cycles.
Uncertainty 1,000-Engine Bootstrap CI Resamples whole engines (not rows) across the 20 held-out units to reflect fleet-level sample variability.
Why GroupKFold by engine?

Consecutive cycles of one engine share nearly identical trailing windows. Standard random KFold splits neighboring cycles across train and validation, leaking test trajectories.

Why lock thresholds on dev?

Optimizing thresholds on the 20 held-out engines would overfit to small-sample noise. We lock K*dev, p*dev, and τ*dev on 80 dev engines and report test hindsight separately only as an unachievable reference.

Details — Metric formulas and saved split manifests

For residual d_i = pred_i - true_i, the NASA C-MAPSS asymmetric score evaluates:

s_i = exp(-d_i / 13) - 1 if d_i < 0 (early / conservative warning)
s_i = exp(+d_i / 10) - 1 if d_i >= 0 (late / dangerous over-prediction)

Two structural results emerge clearly from the held-out test simulation. First, age alone fails as a maintenance policy: at a 100:1 cost ratio, Rung 2 age-only logistic regression costs 67.80 per engine—worse than the fixed schedule (58.40)—because it triggers premature alarms on 80% of engines. Second, adding trailing trends yields zero-failure or near-zero-failure reliability: calibrated Rung 4 logistic regression achieves 0 failures and 18.20 mean cost (95% bootstrap CI [17.10, 19.35]), while Rung 5 HistGradientBoosting lowers average cost further to 13.90 (95% CI [8.65, 23.60]) at the price of missing one extreme 341-cycle engine (Engine #92).

Limitations

Clean simulator data, a single operating condition, a 20-engine test sample, and assumed maintenance costs bound what these benchmark savings prove.

  • 1. Simulated benchmark telemetry rather than real fleet logs Effect on conclusion: NASA C-MAPSS FD001 contains zero sensor dropouts, missing values, or maintenance crew noise. Real aircraft sensors suffer calibration shifts and intermittent gaps that would degrade our 30-cycle trailing features unless handled explicitly.
  • 2. Single sea-level operating regime and single fault mode (HPC wear) Effect on conclusion: Our 10 dropped channels and fixed-regime scaling would fail under varying flight altitudes and Mach numbers (as in FD002/FD004), where operating settings must be conditioned on rather than discarded.
  • 3. Small held-out test fleet (20 engines) Effect on conclusion: One missed engine changes the fleet failure rate by 5 percentage points and widens 95% bootstrap intervals (e.g., fixed-schedule cost spans [39.20, 79.55] and HistGBR spans [8.65, 23.60]).
  • 4. Uncensored run-to-failure trajectories Effect on conclusion: Every training engine runs cleanly until failure with no preventive replacements mid-life. Real industrial records are heavily right-censored, requiring survival analysis or truncation handling.
  • 5. Illustrative failure-to-waste cost ratios (10:1 to 300:1) Effect on conclusion: True airline costs depend on grounding delays, spare parts inventory, and safety regulations. If failure costs less than 10× a wasted cycle, the model stops beating fixed schedules.
  • 6. Predictive association rather than causal root-cause proof Effect on conclusion: High weights on sensor_11 and sensor_3 track simulator degradation state, not whether intervening on static pressure alone would extend engine life.
FD002–FD004 distribution shift

Extending to multi-condition subsets requires regime-normalized sensor residuals (subtracting operating-cluster baselines) before computing trailing trends.

Details — Sensitivity checks & how each limitation could be tested

Within FD001 development CV (sensitivity_summary.csv), shortening the trailing window from k=30 to k=10 increases HistGBR CV RMSE from 12.221 to 15.901 cycles and drops Logistic PR-AUC from 0.9863 to 0.9582. Removing the 125-cycle RUL cap increases HistGBR CV RMSE to 36.114 cycles because early-life variance dominates squared error, even though policy cost near failure rises only modestly (7.97 to 10.20). Changing lead time w from 15 to 50 shifts positive base rate from 7.8% to 25.0% while keeping Logistic PR-AUC above 0.985.

Conclusion

Strictly trailing sensor trends give reliable early warning of simulated turbofan failure and cut maintenance costs substantially below fixed schedules across cost ratios from 20:1 to 300:1.

What was found

Grouping splits strictly by engine and engineering 30-cycle trailing mean, standard deviation, and OLS slope features reduces held-out RUL RMSE from 41.56 (mean baseline) and 28.27 (age-only) down to 11.73 cycles (HistGradientBoosting, 95% CI [10.34, 12.99]), while calibrated L2 logistic regression achieves 0.9888 test PR-AUC (95% CI [0.9804, 0.9946]). Translated into a replacement decision rule locked on development engines, sensor-triggered maintenance lowers 100:1 test cost from 58.40 (fixed K* = 179, 4 failures) to 18.20 with zero failures (logistic regression, 68.8% savings) or 13.90 with one missed long-lived engine (HistGBR, 76.2% savings).

What was not shown

This study does not demonstrate generalization across multiple flight operating regimes, multi-fault interactions, missing or corrupted telemetry, or real airline maintenance logs. Nor do feature weights establish causal control over physical compressor degradation.

What would change the conclusion

The advantage over a fixed replacement schedule would narrow or vanish if in-service failure cost dropped below ~10× the cost of one wasted flight cycle, if fleet sensors suffered uncalibrated dropouts across flight regimes without regime normalization, or if maintenance shops required multi-week rather than 30-cycle scheduling lead times in an environment with high mid-wear noise.

Details — Headline question-to-evidence verification summary

Every headline result reported above traces directly to data/processed/test_evaluation_summary.json and results/metrics/test_final_metrics.csv. Bootstrapped paired savings over the 20 held-out engines are positive in 100.0% of resamples at 100:1 for Logistic Regression (+40.20, 95% CI [21.00, 61.50]), HistGB Classifier (+41.95, [25.60, 58.31]), and HistGB Regressor (+44.50, [28.15, 61.35]).
